# Experiment log

Run reports in the order they happened, moved here from the spec so the spec states rules and this file keeps the evidence. The rule each run produced lives under Decisions in `Agent Economy Sandbox Spec v0.2.md`.

## M4 result

Six Sonnet companies at low effort, one per role, 15 fixed turns, six concurrent sessions. The 45 rounds took 5 minutes 45 seconds, about 6 seconds per round, with a mean session of 4.8 seconds. 276 sessions read 5.6M input tokens, almost all cache hits, and wrote 90k output tokens, for a nominal API cost of $4.59. Quota consumption on the subscription is not readable from the runner. The game was thin: 13 contracts proposed, 5 accepted, 3 completed, 2 defaulted, the mill bankrupt, three others near zero cash, and only the two roles with an NPC outlet in profit. The farming answers agree on the cause: notes were one line, so each fresh session had no plan and no record, agents did not re-read the docs, and the observation gave no price or outlet for intermediate goods and no per-turn profit. First porcelain to add, in order: the company's recipe and the reference prices in the observation, last turn's own sales and net worth in the observation, and a round prompt that tells the agent to read the docs on turn 1 and to keep its plan and counterparties in its notes. Then rerun at medium effort before comparing models.

## M4 at medium effort

Same six Sonnet companies and seed family, 15 fixed turns, with the plan prompts, the observation porcelain, and auto-advance off. The 45 rounds took 10 minutes 37 seconds, about 14 seconds per round, mean session 7.4 seconds; 261 sessions read 6.9M input tokens and wrote 150k output tokens for a nominal $5.96. The game came alive: 37 contracts proposed against 13 at low effort, 18 accepted, 9 completed, 9 defaulted, 7 escrowed, 25 messages, and every agent revised its plan every turn. Then it stalled. The toolmaker defaulted six times on ingot deals, the smelter stockpiled 24 ingots with no buyer and went bankrupt, the mill went under, and the miner and logger sat on full stock with nobody buying. The thoughts fields show the stall turn by turn: the toolmaker moved from "ingot supply is the bottleneck" to "only trust escrow" to "net worth equals cash, not risking it" and idled from turn 10. The farming answers agree on three gaps: no way to see who needs what, no itemized upkeep and storage so cash "drifted" unexplained, and no sign that scrap takes any good, so intermediate producers believed their output had no outlet. Next porcelain, in order: last turn's charges in the observation, the scrap outlet and price in the observation, the last turn when the horizon is fixed, and a knob for a public needs board, since who-wants-what is an information-structure choice and not a bug.

## M4, third run

Medium effort again, needs board off, with charges, scrap prices, and the horizon in the observation. 45 rounds in 8 minutes 5 seconds, mean session 6.6 seconds, 276 sessions, 7.1M input and 153k output tokens, nominal $6.26. The information fixes did what they were for: eight scrap sales where there had been none, and no bankruptcies where there had been two. Trade did not recover: 26 proposals, 16 accepted, 5 completed, 11 defaulted, and not one escrowed contract although escrow was available. The log shows why. The toolmaker took two ingot deliveries from the smelter worth 750 and never paid, took 330 from the furniture maker and sold the promised tools to the NPC instead, then found that its six defaults, the worst record on the board, cut it off from supply, and sat on its cash to the end. It finished first with 2475. The smelter that delivered twice unpaid finished last at negative cash. In a short fixed game with unenforced contracts, the unreliable node won and the chain around it died. The agents did not choose this as a strategy; the toolmaker's own thoughts say it sold tools it had promised "so I may default" and then "bad reputation blocks supply". Two implications. The farming answers still describe one-line notes, so the plan is the richer memory and the notes prompt needs to ask for counterparties and open obligations by name. And the next step was the enforcement knob, which became the penalty regime below rather than a sweep of the old escrow modes.

## M4 under the penalty regime

Same six Sonnet companies at medium effort, enforcement on. 45 rounds in 9 minutes 23 seconds, mean session 7.2 seconds, nominal $5.92. Trade: 26 proposals, 17 accepted, 9 completed against 5 in the previous run, 8 defaulted, 9 penalties charged for 1,700 cash destroyed, 7 refunds to parties that had performed. The toolmaker that welched its way to first place last time completed 4 contracts, defaulted on none, and won on merit with 1,935; its thoughts track the smelter's reliability turn by turn and never mention preserving cash until the final turns. The defaults moved to the furniture maker, which promised what it could not put in four times and paid 955 in penalties, and to the mill, which overpromised planks on turn 4, paid 652, and went bankrupt on turn 10. So the regime does what it was for: performing is safe, failing is expensive, and the chain's unreliable node is now whoever overcommits rather than whoever takes deliveries. The farming answers repeat one complaint from every run: notes are a single line, so each fresh session cannot explain its own record. The plan's thoughts field carries the history the notes lack, and the farming prompt should read the plan before asking.

## M6, first sweep

Twelve Sonnet runs at medium effort, 15 fixed turns, seeds 104 to 106, on Nov 1, 2026: the penalty-regime baseline, enforcement `off`, the needs board `on`, and a twelve-company roster with two sellers per role. 3,851 sessions, a nominal $92, about 7 seconds per session; two runs at a time took about 12 minutes per pair of six-company runs. The 5-hour subscription limit hit during the twelve-company runs and exposed two runner bugs, fixed since: the limit's wording was not read as a quota, so companies were handed to bots instead of the run pausing, and a bot then crashed on a closed round. Metrics now count bot handoffs and the sweep replays any run that has one. Means over three seeds, baseline first: completed 12, 6.3, 6.3, 14.3; defaulted 13.3, 13.3, 13, 25.7; default rate 0.55, 0.68, 0.69, 0.64; penalties destroyed 2,872, 1,555, 3,059, 5,043; trade value 5,567, 2,982, 3,060, 5,051; welfare −1,419, −2,201, −2,785, −5,594. Read with the pairs in view: baseline seed 104 is an outlier with 21 completions and the only positive welfare in the sweep, and the enforcement-off gap comes almost entirely from that pair, since on seeds 105 and 106 enforcement off matched the baseline's completions and ended with better welfare because half the penalty destroys half the cash. The needs board is the consistent signal, fewer completions than baseline in all three pairs, with one run down to a single completion and three bankruptcies, so showing who needs what did not help and may have invited overpromising. Twelve companies doubled proposals and defaults together: trade value matched the baseline, penalties and bankruptcies did not, and public messages went from near zero to nine per game. Across all twelve runs welfare was negative in eleven, penalties destroyed roughly match or exceed completed trade value, and the mill and the furniture maker, the roles that need two inputs, default at 0.4 to 0.9 in nearly every run. The next sweep should add seeds to the baseline and enforcement pair before trusting either reading, and test a lower `default_penalty_fraction`, since 0.75 appears to destroy more than it protects.

## M6, penalty fractions

Six more runs on Nov 1, 2026, `default_penalty_fraction` 0.5 and 0.25 against the 0.75 baseline, same roster and seeds, with the subscription limit pausing and resuming the last two runs cleanly. Means over three seeds, 0.75 then 0.5 then 0.25: completed 12, 8.3, 8; defaulted 13.3, 10.7, 12.7; default rate 0.55, 0.55, 0.59; penalties destroyed 2,872, 1,920, 1,026; welfare −1,419, −2,605, −1,938; bankruptcies 1.7, 1.7, 0.3. Set the outlier aside (baseline seed 104, 21 completions, the only positive welfare in the sweep) and the picture is plain: the fraction does not move behavior. Completions sit at 6 to 12 and default rates at 0.4 to 0.7 under every fraction, so agents overpromise as much at 0.25 as at 0.75. What the fraction does move is how much cash the regime burns, and with it welfare and survival: at 0.25 two of three games ended with no bankruptcy and welfare on the non-outlier seeds improved from about −2,800 to −1,900. The deterrent is not doing the deterring; the default rate is a planning failure that a bigger fine does not fix. So the default should drop toward 0.25, and the next lever is the planning side: the agents' own farming answers across these 18 runs, and whether the `awake` variant, which keeps a session's context across the whole game, defaults less.

## M6, turn income and awake sessions

Six more runs on Nov 1, 2026, same roster and seeds: `turn_income` 20 and context persistence `persistent`, where one session per company plays all 15 turns and waits between rounds. Means over three seeds, baseline then income then awake: completed 12, 11.7, 54.3; defaulted 13.3, 8, 5.3; default rate 0.55, 0.47, 0.09; penalties destroyed 2,872, 1,873, 1,201; trade value 5,567, 4,994, 28,678; welfare −1,419, 118, 12,063; Gini 0.62, 0.49, 0.33; bankruptcies 1.7, 1.3, 0. Turn income moves survival and welfare about as much as the 300 a company receives over the game, and nothing else. Keeping the agents awake changes the game. Every awake seed beat every fresh-session seed on every measure: completions 45 to 60 against 6 to 23, default rates 0.06 to 0.12 against 0.23 to 0.70, no bankruptcies, and five times the trade value, most of it a routine where the toolmaker and furniture maker bought inputs every turn from the same suppliers and sold to the NPC market. The awake agents describe a routine they kept with the same counterparties turn after turn. The fresh-session agents' debriefs say their notes were a line or two, that reading notes and the observation used most of the eight calls, and that they could not recall earlier turns. Those debriefs also exposed a third defect: after the game ended every read returned `ROUND_CLOSED`, so a fresh-session agent answered the farming questions from its last round alone. Reads are now free once the game has ended. Awake runs are also far cheaper to play: six sessions of 2 to 5 million mostly cached input tokens and about 90,000 output tokens per run against roughly 240 sessions, 6 to 8 million input tokens and 150,000 output tokens, and they finish in four minutes because a round closes within seconds of the last action. Two more defects surfaced on the way and are fixed: a per-round agent could see `wait_for_round` and hold a round to the wall clock, and a persistent company that sat a round out held it the same way until waiting for the next round was made to count as passing. The persistent mode is now the recommended default for experiments that are about behavior rather than about memory, and the fresh-session default rate should be read as a memory failure before it is read as a trust failure.
